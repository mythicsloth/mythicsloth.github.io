Hybrid Aerial
Underwater Drone

At Seagrass Robotics, we’re developing a hybrid aerial underwater drone that combines the reach of an aircraft with the underwater movement of a buoyancy driven glider. Our goal is a vehicle that can fly to a site, enter the water, collect data, and return to flight. Bringing these modes together requires closely connected work across the airframe, buoyancy control, waterproof electronics, and system integration.

Berm Building
Lunar Rover

Developed for NASA’s 2026 Lunabotics Competition, ATHENA is designed to turn simulated lunar soil into protective berms. Structures like these could help shield future lunar infrastructure from debris produced during landings and launches. The project explores how robotic excavation, transportation, and deposition can work together to build useful structures from material already available on the Moon.

V3
Excavator System

Regolith lost during transfer limits how much material a rover can ultimately deliver. Our team used a bucket ladder architecture and focused on addressing the leakage and speed tradeoffs seen in earlier designs. I co-led the design and prototyping of V1 and V2, exploring how to retain more material during the handoff to the deposition system. These prototypes formed the basis for V3, which other team members developed.

Behind the design
How does the bucket ladder fit the task?

The bucket ladder combines collection and lifting along a moving series of buckets. Our design work focused on the transfer into deposition, where retaining material was critical to useful throughput.

Why focus on leakage?

Material lost between subsystems cannot be delivered to the end zone. Earlier designs often faced leakage or a compromise in speed; our goal was to retain more regolith without sacrificing operating speed.

V2
Deposition System

The deposition system needed to capture incoming regolith and keep it contained until delivery to the end zone. I designed V1 and V2 around that requirement, prioritizing material retention and capacity within the rover’s constrained design envelope. The aim was to make the available space as useful as possible while minimizing losses between collection and delivery.

Behind the design
What drove the deposition design?

Retention and capacity were the main priorities. The system needed to catch regolith arriving from the excavator and keep it contained until delivery.

How did the size constraint shape the work?

The rover had a permitted boundary box. My work focused on making useful space for regolith within that envelope while accounting for the interfaces with the other subsystems.

ATHENA
System Integration

My work on ATHENA extended to how the subsystems functioned together. Transfers between mechanisms were a central design challenge: material could spill at the interface, while slowing the transfer could limit throughput. I helped integrate the subsystems with a focus on these handoffs, working to maintain a contained material path within the rover’s permitted dimensions.

Behind the design
What made the handoff a system-level challenge?

The excavator’s output and deposition system’s intake had to work together. I worked across the team on these interfaces, with a focus on keeping material contained as it moved between mechanisms.

What tied my contributions together?

Across excavator prototyping, deposition design, and integration, my focus was preserving regolith within the rover’s permitted dimensions so more of the collected material could reach the end zone.

LOOKING BACK

Lessons learned

DRAFT REFLECTION
Design the handoff as part of the system.

Collecting regolith is only useful if it can be retained through transfer and delivery. The challenge on ATHENA highlights why the excavator and deposition system need to be developed around their shared interface, alongside their individual functions.

What to carry into the next design

Prototype the transfer between mechanisms early. Evaluate the material that reaches deposition, the material lost along the way, and the time required for the transfer together.

LOOKING AHEAD

Future design concept

PROPOSED EXPLORATION · UNTESTED
A more enclosed transfer path.

One concept to explore is a guide or shroud around the handoff from the bucket ladder to deposition. The aim would be to direct more regolith into the receiving system while fitting within the rover’s permitted dimensions.

What would need to be tested?

Compare an enclosed handoff with the existing interface using the same material input and operating speed. Measure retained material, spillage, and transfer time, while checking for buildup, jamming, and interference with moving parts. Any retention benefit would need to be weighed against added mass and maintenance access.

BACKGROUND

About Me

I’m a mechanical engineer focused on building persistent ocean intelligence. As cofounder of Seagrass Robotics, I’m developing a hybrid aerial underwater drone to make the ocean more accessible and gather data that deepens our understanding of it.

I believe advances in AI create new possibilities for ocean exploration. At Seagrass, our vision is a hybrid platform that can gather information both above and below the ocean’s surface, moving between air and water without requiring people onsite to deploy or recover it. I believe combining these perspectives with greater autonomy could transform how we explore and understand the ocean.

I enjoy designing and building prototypes that push the boundaries of what’s possible today, turning ambitious ideas into physical systems I can test and improve.
